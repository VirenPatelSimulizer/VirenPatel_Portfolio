Gravitational N-Body Simulation

Interactive gravitational dynamics with thermal energy tracking

What is N-Body Simulation?

An N-body simulation is a computational method that predicts the motion of particles under the influence of physical forces, typically gravity. The term "N-body" refers to the number of objects being simulated, where each object interacts with all others.

This type of simulation was first developed by Erik Holmberg in 1941 using light bulbs to model galactic dynamics. Modern N-body simulations are essential tools in astrophysics, used to study galaxy formation, stellar clusters, and planetary systems.

Physics Behind the Simulation

This simulation models several key physical principles:

  • Gravitational Attraction: Every particle attracts every other particle with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance.
  • Elastic Collisions: When particles collide, momentum is exchanged based on mass ratios. Energy can be lost to thermal energy depending on the elasticity coefficient.
  • Thermal Energy: Kinetic energy lost during inelastic collisions is converted to thermal energy, visualized as a color change from blue (cold) to orange/red (hot).
  • Radiative Cooling: Hot particles gradually lose thermal energy over time, simulating the emission of thermal radiation into space.

Key Equations

Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation:

F = G * (m₁ * m₂) / r²

Where G is the gravitational constant, m₁ and m₂ are the masses, and r is the distance between them.

The simulation uses a softening parameter to prevent numerical instabilities:

F = G * (m₁ * m₂) / (r² + ε²)

Where ε (epsilon) is the softening length, preventing infinite forces when particles get very close together.

Collision energy transfer follows conservation of momentum:

m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'

Simulation Controls

  • Particle Count: Number of gravitating bodies (1-1000). More particles = more complex gravitational interactions.
  • Speed: Initial velocity multiplier for particles. Higher values create more dynamic, energetic systems.
  • Gravity Strength: Controls the gravitational constant G. Higher values cause stronger attraction.
  • Elasticity: Coefficient of restitution (0-1). 1 = perfectly elastic, 0 = perfectly inelastic collisions.
  • Radiation Loss: Rate at which thermal energy dissipates over time, simulating radiative cooling.
  • Thermal Threshold: Maximum thermal energy for color scale. Particles at this value appear fully hot (orange/red).

Thermal Visualization

The simulation includes real-time thermal energy tracking:

  • Color Gradient: Particles transition from blue (cold, low thermal energy) to orange/red (hot, high thermal energy) based on accumulated collision energy.
  • Average Thermal Display: The stats panel shows the average thermal energy across all particles, indicating the overall "temperature" of the system.
  • Energy Conservation: Watch as kinetic energy converts to thermal during collisions, then radiates away over time, demonstrating thermodynamic principles.

Interesting Behaviors to Observe

  • Gravitational Collapse: With high gravity and low initial speed, particles will collapse toward the center of mass.
  • Orbital Systems: Some particles may form temporary orbital pairs or groups around heavier bodies.
  • Thermal Hotspots: Areas with frequent collisions will appear as bright orange clusters, cooling down as particles disperse.
  • Equilibrium States: With balanced parameters, the system may reach a quasi-stable state where average thermal energy stabilizes.
