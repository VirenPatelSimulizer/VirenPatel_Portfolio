Tri-Particle Simulation

Three interacting species with tunable pairwise forces, elastic collisions, and emergent structure

Overview

This simulation extends a classical two-species particle system to three distinct species—labelled A, B, and C. Each species pair has its own tunable force parameter, creating six independent interaction channels. By adjusting these forces from attractive to repulsive, a rich space of emergent behaviors opens up: clustering, segregation, chasing dynamics, and mixed-phase structures.

The physics engine is deliberately simple—inverse-square forces plus hard-sphere collisions—so the complexity you see on screen comes entirely from the combinatorics of three-way interactions, not from any hidden higher-order rules.

Physics Model

Every particle interacts with every other particle through two mechanisms:

  • Inverse-Square Pairwise Forces: Each pair of particles experiences a radial force whose magnitude falls off with the square of the distance. The sign of the force depends on the species pair: negative values produce attraction, positive values produce repulsion.
  • Hard-Sphere Elastic Collisions: When two particles overlap, they are separated and their velocities are updated using an impulse-based collision response. The elasticity coefficient controls how much kinetic energy is conserved—from perfectly elastic (1.0) to perfectly inelastic (0.0).

These two mechanisms are computed every time step. Forces update velocities, velocities update positions, and then overlaps are resolved. Boundary conditions can be set to either solid walls (elastic bounce) or wrap-around (toroidal topology).

Key Equations

The pairwise force between particles i and j is:

F_ij = α(type_i, type_j) · K / r²

Where α is the signed force coefficient for the species pair (negative = attract, positive = repel), K is a global force strength constant (1000), and r is the distance between particle centers. The force is clamped to ±10 to prevent numerical explosions at very small separations.

Velocity update (Euler integration):

v(t + dt) = v(t) + (F / m) · dt

Collision impulse (along the contact normal n):

J = -(1 + e) · (v_rel · n) / (1/m_i + 1/m_j)

Where e is the elasticity coefficient (0–1) and v_rel is the relative velocity of the two particles.

Interaction Matrix

With three species there are six unique pair interactions. The default configuration is:

Pair Default Force Behavior
A–A +0.5 Self-repulsion — A particles spread apart
A–B −0.3 Cross-attraction — A and B cluster together
A–C +0.2 Mild repulsion — A and C avoid each other
B–B +0.5 Self-repulsion — B particles spread apart
B–C −0.5 Strong cross-attraction — B and C bind tightly
C–C +0.3 Self-repulsion — C particles spread apart

The matrix is symmetric—the force that A exerts on B is equal and opposite to the force B exerts on A (Newton's Third Law). This means only 6 parameters are needed rather than 9.

Why Three Species?

Moving from two to three species is not just an incremental change—it is a combinatorial leap. With two species you have 3 force parameters (A-A, A-B, B-B). With three species you have 6. This more than doubles the parameter space and enables qualitatively new phenomena:

  • Frustrated Systems: A attracts B, B attracts C, but A repels C. The system cannot satisfy all interactions simultaneously, leading to dynamic rearrangement and oscillation.
  • Mediator Particles: One species can act as a bridge between two others that would otherwise repel. For example, if A-C repel but both attract B, then B particles form an interface layer.
  • Rock-Paper-Scissors Dynamics: Setting up cyclic chase relationships (A chases B, B chases C, C chases A) produces persistent swirling motion with no equilibrium.
  • Phase Separation: With the right balance of self-repulsion and cross-attraction, you can see distinct "phases" form—regions dominated by one or two species with sharp boundaries.

Controls Reference

  • Particle Counts (A, B, C): Number of each species (10–500). Higher counts create denser, more interactive systems but reduce frame rate.
  • Speed: Initial velocity magnitude for all particles. Higher speeds create more energetic systems where particles can escape local clusters.
  • Radius Scale: Multiplier on particle size (0.5–3.0). Larger particles collide more often and form bonds at greater distances.
  • Collision Elasticity: Energy conservation during collisions (0–1). At 0, collisions are fully inelastic and particles stick together. At 1, collisions are perfectly elastic.
  • Bond Length Factor: Controls the distance threshold for visualizing bonds between attracted particles. Only pairs with negative (attractive) force show bonds.
  • Force Sliders: Six sliders controlling the signed force magnitude for each species pair. Negative values attract, positive values repel. Range: −5 to +5.
  • Wall Mode: "Solid Walls" bounces particles off edges. "Wrap-Around" creates a toroidal space where particles exiting one side re-enter from the opposite side.

Interesting Configurations to Try

  • Universal Attraction: Set all forces to −0.5. All particles collapse into a single dense cluster. Reduce elasticity to 0.2 to see them stick.
  • Species Segregation: Set self-forces (A-A, B-B, C-C) to −1.0 and all cross-forces to +2.0. Each species clusters with its own kind while repelling the others.
  • Cyclic Chase: Set A-B = −2, B-C = −2, C-A = −2, and the reverse pairs (B-A, C-B, A-C) to +2. Since forces here are symmetric this creates mutual attraction in those specific pairs while other pairs repel, producing swirling clusters.
  • B as Glue: A-A = +1, C-C = +1, A-C = +1, but A-B = −2 and B-C = −2, B-B = +0.5. A and C particles only come together when B particles bridge them.
  • Gas-Liquid Transition: Start with all forces at 0 (ideal gas). Slowly increase cross-attraction while keeping self-repulsion. Watch droplets nucleate and grow.

Computational Notes

The simulation uses a brute-force O(N²) approach: every particle checks every other particle for forces and collisions each frame. This is simple and exact, but limits practical particle counts to a few hundred on most devices.

Integration uses the symplectic Euler method with a fixed time step (dt = 0.016s, approximately 60 Hz). Forces are clamped to ±10 to prevent numerical instability when particles approach very closely. Collision resolution uses position correction (separating overlapping particles) followed by impulse-based velocity exchange.

On mobile devices, particle radii are automatically scaled down (75% on tablets, 60% on phones) to maintain visual clarity and performance at smaller screen sizes.

Connections to Real Physics

While this simulation uses simplified inverse-square forces, the three-species interaction model has direct analogies in several real physical systems:

  • Charged Colloids: Mixtures of differently-charged colloidal particles in solution exhibit species-dependent attraction and repulsion, forming crystals and gels.
  • Nuclear Physics: Protons and neutrons interact via the strong force with charge-dependent electromagnetic corrections—a two-force, multi-species system.
  • Ternary Alloys: Three-component metal alloys exhibit phase separation governed by pairwise mixing enthalpies, directly analogous to our force matrix.
  • Ecological Models: Predator-prey-resource dynamics with three trophic levels produce cyclic population oscillations similar to the chasing patterns seen here.
