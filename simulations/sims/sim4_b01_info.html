Linear Aggregates

Self-propelled particles forming worm-like filament structures

What are Linear Aggregates?

Linear aggregates are elongated, chain-like structures that emerge when particles follow simple local bonding rules. In this simulation, self-propelled particles move at constant speed in their orientation direction while interacting with neighbors through short-range forces, creating worm-like filament structures without any global blueprint.

This phenomenon is observed in nature across many scales: from actin filaments in cells to bacterial chains and even animal herding patterns. The key insight is that local alignment and forward-biased attraction are sufficient to produce coordinated linear motion.

Physics Behind the Simulation

This simulation combines active matter physics with collective behavior:

  • Self-Propulsion: Each particle moves at a constant propulsion speed in its current orientation direction, modeling motor-driven motion in biological systems.
  • Angular Noise: Random perturbations to particle orientation simulate thermal fluctuations and prevent perfect alignment, allowing exploration of the environment.
  • Forward-Biased Attraction: Particles preferentially attract neighbors that are ahead of them, creating a head-to-tail linking mechanism that drives chain formation.
  • Orientation Alignment: Nearby particles tend to align their orientations, producing flocking behavior similar to the Vicsek model of collective motion.
  • Short-Range Repulsion: Close-range repulsive forces prevent particle overlap and maintain physical spacing within chains.

Key Equations

Particle velocity is determined by its orientation angle:

v = propulsion_speed × (cosθ, sinθ)

Orientation evolves through alignment with neighbors plus noise:

θ(t+dt) = θ(t) + alignment_torque + η(t)

Where η(t) is angular noise drawn from a uniform distribution, and the alignment torque depends on the average orientation of neighbors within the alignment radius.

Simulation Controls

  • Propulsion Speed: Self-propulsion velocity (10-200). Higher speeds create more dynamic, extended filaments.
  • Angular Noise: Random orientation perturbation (0-0.3). Low noise = stable chains, high noise = dispersed particles.
  • Interaction Radius: Range of short-range forces (20-150). Controls how far particles can "sense" each other.
  • Repulsion Radius: Close-range repulsion distance (5-40). Prevents particle overlap.
  • Forward Bias: Preference for forward-directed attraction (0-1). Higher values produce more linear chains.
  • Orientation Alignment Strength: Flocking tendency (0-0.2). Controls how strongly particles align with neighbors.
  • Alignment Radius: Sensing distance for orientation alignment (10-100).

Interesting Behaviors to Observe

  • Worm-like Filaments: With moderate forward bias and alignment, particles self-assemble into elongated chains that slither through space like worms.
  • Swirling Vortices: At certain parameter combinations, chains curl into rotating vortex patterns, demonstrating collective rotation.
  • Chain Splitting & Merging: Filaments can break apart and reconnect, creating a dynamic network of living chains.
  • Disordered-to-Ordered Transition: Gradually reduce angular noise to watch the system transition from a disordered gas to ordered filaments.
